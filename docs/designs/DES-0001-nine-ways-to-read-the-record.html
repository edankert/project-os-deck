Nine ways to read the record, and a tenth

The cockpit has one shape — three panes and a document in the middle — and it is the right shape for reading a record and discharging what it owes. These are ten other shapes the same corpus supports. Each is pitched as a whole application with its own navigation — the only window you would open — not a theme over the existing one. The matrix below is how that claim is checked, and the coverage table after it is where four of the ten admit they cannot carry everything.

Almost nothing here is accepted, and one thing is. ORBIT was asked for and is filed as FEAT-0001 with five tasks; its visual treatment is an open decision drawn three ways. Everything else is a proposal with no feature, task or phase against it. Two designs argue against decisions this project has already taken — ASK against PHASE-028's own out-of-scope list, DECK against ADR-0020 — and each says so on its own plate. ROOM is plate 10 because it was cut, challenged, and re-drawn. Every figure in the mocks is measured from this repo.

They differ where it counts

Six axes. Two designs sharing a row would be one design with two skins, so this table is the first thing to attack. The one that matters most is who decides the layout: an auto-laid graph, a fixed map, a queue that shows you one thing, and a table you arrange yourself are four genuinely different relationships with the same 1537 notes.

01 ORBIT three ways to be inside the corpus

16148 wikilinks over 1537 notes is a structure nobody in this project has ever seen. The cockpit resolves one link when you click it and lists backlinks at the foot of a note — the local view of a shape never drawn whole. ORBIT draws it and gives you no list at all: you steer, and the corpus has an inside. As a whole application it keeps the cockpit's reader for reading and replaces everything around it. As a look it can go three genuinely different ways, and the toggle below is the real decision — they disagree about what the view is claiming, not just about colour.

Three buttons, one dataset, one palette — the status bands are the implementation's in all three. What changes is the claim: a sky you are lost in, an instrument you are operating, or an object on a table you are looking at from outside.
CONSTELLATION
what I had in mind first

Astronomical, and quiet. Points of light in near-black, filaments so faint you read them as texture, depth by haze. No chrome at all — the sky is the interface, and the only UI is the small type in the corners.

It argues that the corpus is vast and mostly settled, which is true: 70% of it is terminal work. The risk is that it is beautiful and undifferentiated — every region looks like every other region, and "beautiful, then never opened again" is the standard fate of this view.

GLASS
the Minority Report reading

An instrument, not a scene. Cyan on black, nodes as glass plates rather than points, brackets and reticles, billboarded panels floating at depth, a scan sweep, tick rules down the edges. Everything looks like it is being operated.

It argues that the corpus is a live system you are working. It also carries the most risk of the three: this is a costume, and a governance tool that looks like a film prop invites you to trust the graphic rather than the data.

It costs a distinction, and the mock shows it. Washing every band toward one instrument colour puts four of the six statuses inside 25 degrees of hue — so blocked is held out as the single alarm red, and archived is desaturated to keep it readable. Two of the six survive as themselves. That is a real loss on a surface whose first job is to say what state something is in.

BLOCKS
a made thing on a table

Opaque wooden solids on a lit floor, stacked into districts by phase, height by inbound links, casting real shadows. Warm, physical, and lit rather than glowing.

Finished work is the bare wood. 70% of this corpus is terminal, so painting it would produce a city of one colour; instead done is the unpainted material and only the live statuses are painted blocks. The city is mostly beech with a scatter of red and green, which is what the record actually looks like.

It argues that the record is a made object with weight, and occlusion becomes honest information — a block behind another really is hidden. The cost is the edges: 16148 filaments do not survive an opaque scene, so the link callout, ORBIT's best idea, goes with them.

The whole application

At rest there is no chrome — the field fills the window and nothing else is on screen. That is the bet: 1537 notes have a shape, and you arrive by recognising where you are rather than by reading a tree.

The document is not reinvented. Landing on a node opens the cockpit's existing reader in a panel over the field, with wikilinks, backlinks and tickable checkboxes intact. Dismiss it and you are back where you were flying, not back at a home screen.

The verbs stay with their subject. A node that owes something wears a halo and the count sits in the corner — but you discharge it on the note, because a verdict made without the note around it is a verdict made without its context.

The terminal is a slab that materialises in front of the field on ⌘`, dark and rectangular against the depth, deliberately not styled to match. It is a real shell; the field dims behind it and stops moving. In BLOCKS it reads as a workbench drawer pulled out over the table — same panel, different furniture.

The fleet is the honest weak point. Twelve repos means twelve fields, and one sky containing all of them needs a process that can see every repo — exactly what FEAT-0093 says does not exist. The rail survives, as twelve distant lights you jump between.

Navigation

No tree, no list, no back button in the usual sense. You fly: drag to turn, scroll to close in, double-click to land. ⌘K flies the camera rather than cutting, so a note arrives from a direction and you keep your bearings.

BLOCKS changes even this — you orbit an object on a table rather than moving through a volume, which is easier to hold in your head and much harder to get lost in.

Data it needs

Exists: wikilinks.py resolves every target; the index holds every note's frontmatter; status bands come from statuses.py.

New: the whole edge list in one payload with each link's character offset — TASK-0001 — and a layout computed once and kept, TASK-0002, which is the harder half and the one that decides whether the field is worth learning.

Filed as FEAT-0001, since you asked for this one to be real.

The objection

Graph views are famously beautiful and famously useless. Obsidian's is the standard example: people open it, say "wow", and never navigate with it.

Its candidate answer is structural — what nothing links to, which clusters hang by a single edge, where a note sits two hops out. The first is a query a table could answer. The other two are not, and that is the whole case.

The treatment carries its own risk. GLASS is the one to watch: the look is borrowed from a film about a system that was confidently wrong.

02 ATLAS land you learn by heart

ORBIT moves and ATLAS never does. Every note has a permanent address, computed once and then frozen: FEAT-0143 is in the east, on the coast, next to the harbour where the fleet work landed, and it will be there next month and next year. That is the whole idea — a map you can remember, so finding something stops being a search and becomes a recollection. Regions are phases, and the map shows two things no list does: how much of the territory is old, drawn as fog, and where the record simply stops.

The whole application

At rest the map fills the window, the inset holds the whole territory, and the address panel names the region you are looking at. Nothing else is on screen.

The document docks to the right as a field office: the note in the cockpit's own renderer, the map dimmed but not moved. Losing your place is the one thing a map may never do.

The verbs are pins. A district with owed work carries a marker cluster with a count, and zooming in resolves it into individual pins — which is the map's native answer to "obligations live with their subject", since a pin is at the thing.

The terminal rises from the bottom edge as a drawer, the way a maps application shows directions: full width, fixed dark, half the height. The map stays put above it.

The fleet is this design's best feature. Twelve repos are an archipelago, and the cross-repo links that FEAT-0093 has to carry as payloads become shipping lanes between islands. One picture, twelve sovereign sidecars — the only design here where the fleet's real architecture and the metaphor agree.

Navigation

Pan and zoom, exactly like a map application, plus the two things maps have that graphs do not: a permanent address and an inset that never loses the whole. You navigate by remembering where a thing was, not by searching for what it is called.

Places can be bookmarked as coordinates, so "the corner where the release work is" is a link you can send someone.

Data it needs

Exists: phases, every note's phase:, statuses, and updated: on every note — fog is just a date threshold over data the index already holds.

New: a layout pass that assigns coordinates and stores them, plus the rule for what happens when a note is added: it must take a plot in its region without moving its neighbours, or the map's whole promise breaks.

The objection

Frozen coordinates rot. A corpus that grew 634 notes in a single month will overflow whatever plots it was given, and the moment the map re-flows to fit, every address a person memorised is wrong — worse than never having promised one.

The fix is real work, not a detail: plots must be allocated with room to grow, and a region that fills up has to spill into new land at the edge rather than re-drawing the interior.

Every note carries created: and updated:, every change note is dated in its filename, and the snapshot has been committed on every change since January. The whole application

The fleet is a track group per repo, collapsed by default. Twelve expanded at once is unreadable, and admitting that is better than a picture that only works with three.

Navigation

An editor's transport, borrowed whole: J back, K stop, L forward, hold to shuttle faster. I and O set in and out points. You navigate by time, never by name — the only view here where "the week the fleet migration happened" is a first-class address.

Selecting a span and pressing ⌥R drafts the change note for everything inside it, which is the close-out step done from evidence rather than from memory.

Data it needs

Exists: every date the mock uses is in frontmatter today, and git_state.py already reads the commit history the clips would be cut against.

New: a state-at-date query. Frontmatter records the current status, not when it changed, so the honest reconstruction has to come from git log over the notes — which is real work and the reason this is the most expensive of the nine.

The objection

Nobody asks what the project looked like in July. Time-travel views are built more often than they are used, and this one is the costliest to build honestly.

The counter is the span, not the instant: "what happened between these two dates" is asked at every close-out and every release, and today it is answered by reading commit messages. If REEL is built, it should be built for the selection, and the scrub is a side effect.

Navigation

There is none, and that is the design. Nothing is nested, nothing scrolls, and no state is more than one keystroke away. It is built to be looked at from four feet away on a second monitor while you work on something else.

Interaction is intervention, never browsing: approve, stop, take over, mute. A verb that needs a second screen to complete does not belong here.

Data it needs

Exists, all of it. agent_hooks.py already records agent, session, prompts, files, docs_notes, cost, context percentage and the undocumented flag; the fleet already runs one sidecar per repo.

The objection

An instrument wall for one person running two agents is cosplay. Most of this screen is empty most of the time, and a dashboard that is usually idle trains you to stop looking at it — which is worse than not having it when the one real alarm fires.

The honest version of PULSE may be much smaller: the alarm row alone, delivered as a notification, with no wall at all.

05 ASK the record, answering

1537 notes is past the size where a person can find an answer by reading. Half the corpus is reasoning — why the ledger is JSON, why the sweep was withdrawn, why a verdict is an event — and that reasoning is exactly what a new session, or a returning human, needs and cannot locate. ASK puts a prompt in front of it: the answer is prose with citations, every claim traceable to the note it came from, and the two moves borrowed from t3.codes are forking a thread and switching the model mid-conversation so two readings of the same question sit side by side.

The whole application

At rest there is a prompt, a thread tree, and the shelf. The corpus is not displayed at all until you ask — which is the appeal at 1537 notes and the risk for anyone new, who has no idea what can be asked.

The terminal is the sharpest version of that problem. The fleet is free. One question spans twelve repos, because retrieval does not care which sidecar owns a note.

The objection

Proposing it anyway needs a reason that decision was wrong. The candidate: that phase was about driving agents, and this is about reading the record — a different thing that happens to share a text box. That may be a real distinction or a convenient one, and it is Edwin's call, not mine.

06 DECK one thing, then the next thing

Every other design here shows you more. DECK shows you less, on purpose: one obligation, filling the screen, with no way to see the queue behind it and no way to browse. You answer it or you defer it, and the next one comes up. The measured case for it is blunt — 70 notes in this repo carry a reviewer's changes-requested and 61 of them are already at a terminal status, a backlog that grew by 21 in the sixteen days since it was last written down. The whole application

At rest there is one card, and no way to see the queue behind it. Everything below is a consequence of that single decision.

The document is the card. A card carries the whole subject — the note's text, its evidence, the reviewer's words — and a card that cannot is a card that opens the note instead. That rule is what keeps this from violating ADR-0020 outright, and it is still an argument rather than a settled point.

Sized for a phone or a tablet on the sofa, because the state it is designed for is "ten minutes, not at my desk".

Data it needs

Exists: obligations.py already computes what is owed and approvals.py already knows which verbs are human-only. The stale-verdict rule already ships as REVIEW-STALE in the validator.

New: an ordering — the hard part. A queue that deals the wrong card first is worse than a list, because you cannot see past it to the one that mattered.

The objection

ADR-0020 says obligations live with their subject, not in a central queue you visit. DECK is precisely the central queue that decision rejected, and the reason it was rejected still holds: a verdict made without the note around it is a verdict made without its context.

The narrow defence is that a card is not a queue-with-a-list — it can carry the whole subject, and a card that cannot is one that should open the note instead. Whether that satisfies ADR-0020 or evades it is the argument to have before anyone builds this.

The objection

A general tool answers every question badly. A table that can express anything expresses nothing in particular — which is why the cockpit's opinionated views exist, and why "add a filter for that" is the answer that has quietly killed a hundred good products.

There is also a real hazard: bulk edit makes it easy to set forty statuses at once, and a status set in bulk is a status nobody thought about.

You watch first, then click a station to open its queue. Dragging a parcel to another station is a status transition — the gesture and the write are the same act, which is either the most direct interface here or the most dangerous.

10 ROOM the record at body scale

This one was cut, and then asked for. It sat in the not-drawn list with the reason "the hardware is not on the desk, and a headset view whose advantage over ORBIT is bigger is not a different application" — which was too quick. Standing up is a different application: at body scale the corpus stops being a picture you look at and becomes a place you are in, with a behind you, and things you have to turn around to see. That changes what you notice. It also changes almost nothing about reading a note, which is the honest limit. Three treatments below; they are not skins, they disagree about where you are.

THE STACKS
what I had in mind, drawn properly this time

You walk down a hall. Each bay is a phase; the documents are lit panels on the walls at reading height; the far end of the hall is the far end of the project. Depth is chronology — walk forward and you walk into last spring.

Its argument is scale you can feel. 1537 notes is an abstraction on a screen and a long corridor in a room, and the length of that corridor is the only honest picture of the corpus anyone has had. Its weakness is that it is a metaphor with nothing under it: a hall is not how the notes are organised, and any bay you walk into could equally have been a list.

GESTURE ROOM
the Minority Report reading

You stand still and the record comes to you. Panes hang in an arc at arm's length; you pull one closer, push one away, throw one aside. Nothing is on a wall — the arrangement is yours, and it collapses when you leave.

Its argument is manipulation over navigation, and it is the treatment that fits this project's actual work: comparing four notes at once, which no flat screen does well. Its weakness is the famous one — gorilla arm. The film never showed anyone doing that for six hours, and the review backlog is a six-hour job.

BUILD TABLE
play blocks, at room scale

The record is a model on a table and you walk around it. Same wooden blocks as ORBIT, now at 1:1 in a room: you lean in, pick a block up, turn it over, set it back. Someone else can stand on the other side.

Its argument is the only one of the three with a second person in it. A table is a shared object; a headset view of a corridor is not. If this project's problem is that judgment sits with one human, a surface built for two people around a model is the one that changes something. It is also the least immersive, which is probably why it is the most usable.

The whole application — and where it stops

At rest you are standing in it. There is no window, no rail, no tab bar; the fleet's twelve repos are twelve doors, or twelve tables in one workshop.

The document is the wall. This is where the honesty is needed: reading 1500 words of Markdown in a headset is worse than reading it on a laptop, and no amount of design fixes that. The Stacks can show a note as a lit panel and you can read a heading and a status from it, but the reader you would actually use is still the flat one.

The verbs work well, and this is the surprise. Approve, triage, defer and decide are one-gesture acts, and a gesture is a good fit for a verdict: pick it up, look at it, put it in one of two places. The Build Table's version — sliding a block across to the other side — is the clearest discharge gesture in this whole document.

The terminal cannot come. A shell is a keyboard surface; a virtual keyboard at body scale is a joke, and passing through a real one means you are looking at a desk through a headset. So ROOM is the one design here that must say plainly: the terminal stays on the machine, and you take the headset off to use it.

Which is why it is a companion, not a replacement. It cannot host the reader or the shell. What it can host is the part of the work that is judgment rather than typing.

You turn your head, and you walk. The Stacks navigates by going somewhere; the Gesture Room does not navigate at all, it arranges; the Build Table navigates by walking around a thing — three different verbs that a flat screen renders identically as "scroll".

New: a WebXR surface, a hand-tracking interaction layer, and a text-rendering approach that stays legible at 1.5 metres. Also a decision this repo has never had to make: what a second person in the session is allowed to do.

The objection

The original cut still mostly stands. The hardware is not on the desk, the terminal cannot come, the reader is worse, and the sessions are twenty minutes long because of the headset, not because of the work.

What changed my mind is narrower than the pitch: the Build Table is a two-person surface, and every governance decision in this project currently has exactly one person available to make it. That is worth a prototype on its own — and it does not need a headset, only a table and a projector.

Six more, considered and not drawn

Each was a candidate for a slot. The reason it lost is more useful than the concept — and one of the original seven, room-scale VR, was cut here, challenged, and is now plate 10. The cut was too quick, which is the argument for writing these down rather than just not drawing them.

Sonification

The repository as a score — a tone per commit, a chord per phase, dissonance when the validator fails. Cut because sound carries about one bit and cannot be re-read. It is a notification channel wearing an interface's clothes.

The ambient garden

A second screen where the project grows or wilts with its health. Cut on this project's own rule: a decorative surface cannot say "unknown", and DESIGN.md is explicit that unknown and fine are different sentences. A wilting plant is also a guilt machine, which is not governance.

The detective board

A freeform canvas where you pin notes and draw the string — the only layout model none of the nine uses, since every one of them decides the arrangement for you. Cut narrowly, and it is the strongest of the seven. The doubt: the corpus already has an authored link graph, and hand-drawn string would quietly compete with related:.

The pager

Push notification, one-tap approve, nothing else. Cut because FEAT-0079 already owns supervision from a phone. Drawing it here would restate a planned feature as though it were a new idea.

The effort Sankey

Ribbons showing where the work actually went, phase by phase. Cut on data: nothing in this corpus records effort. The ribbons would be item counts drawn as though they were hours, which is a chart that lies about its own units.

The blame reader

A note rendered with every line tinted by when — and by which session — it was written. Cut because it is a feature of the reader that exists, not an application beside it. It belongs in the cockpit's document pane, and it is a good idea there.

If anything gets built

Nine proposals is not a plan, and picking is yours. This is the order I would argue for, and the reasoning is about cost and about what each one would teach, not about which is most interesting to draw.

  1. LEDGER, because it is nearly free and it measures demand for the other eight

    Every field it needs is already parsed and in memory; it is a table over the index. Build it read-only — no bulk edit, none of the risk — and then watch which saved views get used. A view somebody re-runs weekly is a purpose-built surface waiting to be designed, and that is a far better way to choose among the other eight than my opinion or yours.

  2. REEL's selection, without REEL's scrub

    "What happened between these two dates" is asked at every close-out and every release, and today it is answered by reading commit messages. That half needs the git-derived history the expensive half also needs — so build the query, answer the real question with it, and only draw the timeline if the query turns out to be worth looking at.

  3. Then one exploratory one, chosen for the argument it settles

    ORBIT and ATLAS answer the same underlying question — what is the shape of 16148 links — and disagree about whether motion or permanence is the way to show it. Building either one settles that. Building both would be building the same thing twice, and the matrix at the top is where you check that claim.

  4. And two of them need a decision before a line is written

    ASK contradicts PHASE-028's out-of-scope list and DECK contradicts ADR-0020. Neither should be prototyped "to see how it feels" — that is how a recorded decision gets overturned by a demo rather than by an argument. If either is worth building, the ADR changes first.

One thing this exercise found that is not a design

Drawing DECK and FOUNDRY needed a real number for the review backlog, so it was measured. 70 notes now carry a reviewer's changes-requested, and 61 of them are at a terminal status. CLAUDE.md records that measurement as 49 and 43, taken on 20 August — so the backlog grew by 21 in sixteen days, and the file that describes the problem is now understating it.

That belongs in an issue, not in a design dossier, and it holds whether or not any of these nine is ever built.
