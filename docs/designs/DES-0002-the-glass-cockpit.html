The glass cockpit

Depth is priority. The cockpit's left pane is a list, and a list has one dimension — so everything competes for vertical position, which is why ADR-0025 had to be written down as a rule. Here the same three bands every view already computes become near, middle and far: what needs a person is at the front plane, the view's own subject sits in the mid field, and the finished 70% of the corpus recedes into fog. A view is not a page you navigate to; it is a re-arrangement you watch happen.

The rule the whole thing rests on

This is not a metaphor imposed on the data. nav_payload prepends _needs_you_group to all eleven modes and appends suppressed_group to most of them — every view in the cockpit is already three bands. A list can only express that by putting one above another. Depth expresses it directly, and frees vertical position to mean something else.

And the tension this immediately creates

Open four panels in the prototype and the field empties out — the obstacle rule does its job and there is nowhere left to deal a card. Panels and the field compete for the same pixels, and on one monitor that competition has no good resolution.

That is not a bug to tune away. It is the argument for the next section.

The one that breaks the rule, and why it is labelled

Recent uses distance for age instead of obligation. Every other view means the same thing by "in front of you", and a surface that quietly changes what its main axis measures is exactly the kind of thing DESIGN.md's first rule forbids. So Recent says so, in the panel, every time you open it. The alternative — dropping the view — loses the only answer to "what has been happening", which is the question a returning human actually asks.

The console is furniture

Where it sits

Bottom centre by default, because that is where a console has been on every terminal since VT100s, and because the front plane is above eye level in this layout so the two do not fight.

Drag it by the title bar to anywhere in the window; drag the corner to resize. Both are remembered per workspace, like every other pane position the shell already stores.

Why it is transparent

Not for looks. A console covers the part of the field you are working on — you opened it because of something in front of you — so the field has to stay readable through it. The slider in the mock is real: at 20% you can read the notes underneath, at 95% you cannot.

The deep field is allowed to sit under the glass. The front plane is not — see the next column.

The obstacle rule

Slots under the console are removed from the pool before any note is dealt into one. Nothing is nudged aside after the fact, because nudging is how cards land on their neighbours — which is precisely the bug this prototype had and shows in its own commit history.

So moving the console re-deals the front and mid bands around its new footprint, and the quiet band ignores it entirely.

Consoles, sessions and workspaces are one mechanic

The carousel

Every running thing is a card on an arc at the lower left: a workspace shell, a Claude session, a Codex session, a session waiting for approval. The front card is the one the slab is showing.

That single rule replaces two separate places in today's cockpit — the agents panel, which lists sessions, and the terminal panel, which shows one shell. Bringing a card forward is the same act as attaching the console to it.

A session that needs you does not wait its turn: it rotates itself to the front and the slab title turns amber. That is the undocumented flag and the approval prompt given somewhere to appear.

The workspace ring

Twelve repos on a slow ring at the upper left, edge-on, the current one facing you. Each slab carries its owed count, so the fleet's attention is legible without visiting it — which the current rail cannot do.

Same gesture as the carousel, wider radius. Learned once.

The honest limit: those counts come from twelve separate sidecars, and one of them being slow or down has to look different from one being quiet. Absent is not zero — this project has learned that three times.

Selecting a note

The card comes to the front plane and opens. Every note it links to, and every note linking to it, flies into a ring around it with a filament drawn to each. Everything else drops back and dims to almost nothing.

In the mock, opening FEAT-0143 pulls in twelve real neighbours — the ADRs it rests on, the phase that contains it, the tasks under it and the tests that cover it. That is the corpus's 16148 links finally doing work in the reading surface.

The body renders with the cockpit's own renderer. The ring is new; the reader is not.

Turning around, and putting things away

The field is a cylinder, not a wall. Drag the background — or press ← and → — and you turn on the spot; the compass says which way you are facing and how many notes are out of sight behind you. This is what makes the quiet band honest: it is not a dimmed pile cluttering the working view, it is genuinely out of the way, and it is genuinely still there.

Why behind, and not just far

70% of this corpus is terminal. Rendered small and blurred in front of you, that is still 1000 rectangles of visual noise between you and nine things that matter.

Behind you it costs nothing and is one gesture away. "Look behind" is a button, and the count is always on screen so you can never quietly lose track of what has been put there.

Putting one away by hand

An open card offers put behind me. It is the direct-manipulation form of the thing the cockpit already computes as suppressed_group — "not now, and I know it exists".

It is per-session and never a write. Quieting a note in a view must not change the note, or the gesture becomes a status change nobody recorded a reason for.

What it costs

Anything out of sight can be forgotten, and this design has just built a machine for putting things out of sight. The counter and the compass are the whole mitigation, and they may not be enough.

The test is simple and worth running before building: after a week, does anyone press "look behind"?

Many monitors

You are right that this wants more than one screen — and the panel-versus-field competition above is the proof. The proposal is not "the window can be dragged onto a second monitor". It is that the four surfaces have genuinely different refresh rates and attention costs, and each wants a screen of its own.

Screen 1 — the field

The record, the view rail, the compass, the workspace ring. Nothing that scrolls, nothing that updates on its own. This is the screen you turn in.

Freed of panels, the front and mid bands get their full angular budget back — three columns a side instead of one.

Screen 2 — the consoles

Every shell and session, tiled rather than carouselled, because a second monitor has room to show five at once. Pulse is this screen at rest; a console expands out of a lane when you attach to it.

This is the screen that moves. Keeping it off the reading screen is the point.

Screen 3 — the reader and the lists

Opened notes and list panels. Where a list beats a field it gets a whole display, and the field stays a field.

A laptop alone collapses all three into one window, panels and all — which works, and is worse. The design should degrade to that, not be built for it.

What multi-monitor actually costs to build

One state, several windows. Electron gives you extra BrowserWindows cheaply; the hard part is that yaw, view, open set and pushed set now live in a process that no window owns. The shell already has this problem solved for workspaces — the main process holds the state and each renderer subscribes — so this extends an existing pattern rather than inventing one.

The genuinely new question is what "in front of you" means when there are three fronts. My answer: only screen 1 has a front plane. The others are surfaces, not fields, and they do not get a depth axis. Two screens both claiming to show what needs you is how a person ends up trusting neither.

The measurement changed my mind about the look, not just the cost

I expected to be trading appearance for speed. The cheap version reads better. A blurred card is unreadable and still occupies a full card's worth of space, so the quiet band becomes 52 smeared rectangles of text you cannot use. Dropping the title turns each one into a small ID tile, and the fog gradient does the receding — so the far field becomes a texture of identifiers instead of a wall of noise.

That is aerial perspective, which is how distance actually looks: things lose contrast and detail, they do not go out of focus. Blur was simulating a camera, and there is no camera here.

Rev 5 got the detail level backwards and rev 6 fixed it. Distance was dropping the face — the progress bar and the severity chip — and keeping the title. That is exactly wrong: a bar is a rectangle and a chip is one word, both of which survive being small, while a sentence does not. The far field now reads as identifier, status and progress, which is more information than the blurred version carried and less work than either.

One more thing the measurement showed, which no amount of reasoning would have: the blur cost is nearly zero while you face the front. The quiet band is behind you, so the expensive band is off-screen most of the time — and the cost only arrives at the moment you turn, which is the moment the interface most needs to stay smooth.

The desk: as many notes as you need, stacked

Opening a note used to mean "the field re-arranges around it", which does not survive a second note and does not survive at all past three. The fix is to stop treating it as one surface. There are two, and conflating them was the whole problem.

The field

Every note, arranged by the view's rule. It re-arranges when you switch views, because the arrangement is the view's opinion, not yours.

Nothing on it is "open". It is the place things are when you are not holding them.

The desk

The notes you picked up — one desk per view. Switch to Issues and you get the Issues desk; switch back and Publication's five notes are exactly where you left them, untouched.

It survives a workspace switch, though, and the difference is the whole rule: the desk belongs to the job, the view is the job, and the workspace is only which repo you happen to be looking at.

So "opening" is lifting

A note is taken out of the field and put on the desk. Its slot stays behind, ghosted — a dashed outline where it came from — so you can see the hole and put it back.

Nothing is recorded. A note on the desk has no status, owes nothing, and closing it is not an action anyone needs to confirm.

What I would still argue about

One desk per view, surviving workspace switches. Rev 7 had this backwards — it kept one desk across every view, which turns switching into clutter you have to clean up. Edwin: "each view should allow for its own selected notes", and he is right, because the two boundaries are not the same kind of thing.

The view is the job. Triaging issues, closing a phase, preparing a release — different work, different notes, and the notes for one job have no business on another job's desk. The workspace is not a job, it is which repo you are looking at, so a job that spans repos is ordinary. That is why the desk survives one boundary and not the other.

It still does not violate FEAT-0093: the desk holds notes from several repos, but each note's content still comes from its own sidecar. No process queries across repos — the desk is a place to put things, not a joined index. Keeping it alive while pooling

Pooling costs animation, and a field that snaps is a dead field. But the two are only in conflict if you pool the wrong band — and the animation budget is bounded by the visible arc, not by the corpus. Fifty cards moving is nothing; you never have fifteen hundred on screen.

rulewhat it protects
pool only the far bandThe near bands are never recycled. The front holds ~12 and the mid ~40 — bound to their notes for as long as they are near, so they animate freely between views. The quiet band is where the thousand live and where nobody follows an individual card. You get full motion exactly where it is watched, and pooling exactly where the count is
recycle only off-screenA card about to animate is on screen by definition, so it is never a recycling candidate. This is what makes the rule above safe rather than hopeful
cross-fade, never cross-morphWhen a recycle is unavoidable, sliding element #37 from FEAT-0143's position to ISS-0260's is a lie about a note that never moved. Fade it out as one and in as the other — 120ms, opacity only, compositor-only
FLIP for view switchesMeasure first and last positions, apply the inverted transform, then play. The standard technique, and it works with a pool as long as identity is stable within the frame — which rule two guarantees
stagger, do not synchroniseCards settle in a wave from where you are facing outward. This is in the prototype now and costs nothing at all — it is a transition-delay, not extra work. Everything arriving at once reads as a jump; the same motion staggered reads as a field settling

Why this is not a compromise

Transform and opacity transitions run on the compositor — they do not touch layout or paint. The cost is per animating element on screen, and the arc caps that at about 55. So the honest position is not "pool and accept a dead interface"; it is that the interface can only ever animate what you can see, and pooling changes nothing about that number.

What pooling genuinely removes is the continuity of a card that leaves and returns. Turn 180° and back, and the card that comes into view may be a different element than the one that left. Nobody can perceive that — but it means you cannot rely on element identity for anything that must persist, which is the rule already stated: opened and pinned cards live outside the pool.

What pooling actually means here

A fixed set of DOM elements, recycled across a much larger set of notes. The field holds about 55 cards at the widest; the corpus holds 1537. So you build roughly 120 card elements once and re-point them at different notes as you turn, the way a scrolling list reuses row elements. Nothing else about the design changes — but several things stop being free, and they are worth knowing before rather than after.

what it giveswhat it takes awaythe rule that follows
constant cost — 120 elements whether the repo has 200 notes or 7,400 across the fleet. Memory, style recalc and paint stop scaling with the corpus, which is the whole point
— animation between states If element #37 was FEAT-0143 and becomes ISS-0260, there is nothing to animate between — it must snap. So: only recycle an element after it has left the visible arc, never one mid-transition. The "watch a note change importance when you switch views" effect only survives for cards that stay bound
— browser find-in-page ⌘F can only find text that exists in the DOM, so it would search ~120 cards out of 1537 and silently miss the rest. The application must own search — which it does — and must never let the browser's own find look like it worked
— the accessibility tree A screen reader sees the pool, not the corpus. The list panel stops being a convenience and becomes the accessible view — which is a good outcome, but only if it is built that way deliberately
— per-note element state Anything attached to a card element — an open editor, a text selection, a scroll position — dies when the element is reused. So opened, pinned and neighbour cards live outside the pool and get dedicated elements. The pool holds only closed cards in the field
— "just focus that note" A note that is not materialised has no element to focus or scroll to. Placement must precede focus: fly first, then open — which is already how the search box works, and is now a requirement rather than a flourish

The one-line version

Pooling buys unbounded corpus size and costs you the browser's built-in affordances. Find-in-page, the accessibility tree and element-attached state all assume the DOM is the data. Once it is a window onto the data, the application has to supply each of those itself — and a design that skips that is one that works beautifully for a sighted user with 80 notes and fails everyone else at 1537.

Should this be right before anything is built?

You are right that you could list many more defects. That is the most useful signal in this document, and it deserves a straight answer rather than more polish.

What the mock has been good for

Four things it produced that reading the code alone would not: the depth mechanic came out of nav_payload, not the metaphor; the quiet band belongs behind you, which only became obvious once you could turn; blur costs 52 passes over 821k px², measured, and the cheap version reads better; and three real interaction bugs, two of which you found.

That is a good return. It is also mostly spent.

The triage rule I would use

A defect is a design question if the answer changes what you would build. It is polish if a real implementation fixes it for free.

Your two were design questions, and both are now answered: a wire anchors at the card's edge, on the bearing of its neighbour; and an open card owns an exclusion zone — neighbours flank it in columns rather than ringing it, which clears it by construction instead of by nudging.

"The hover state is a bit dim" is polish. Do not spend the mock on it.

So: stop here

Stop when the next defect is polish — which is roughly now. Three of the last four fixes were fights with the prototype's own scaffolding rather than with the design: hand-rolled projection, a measurement environment that lies about frozen transitions, synthetic clicks that skip hit-testing.

Those are not questions about the interface. They are the cost of simulating a renderer instead of using one.

What I would build instead of more mock

Not "the glass cockpit". The narrow slice every version of this needs, which only real data can validate: a read-only field view inside the cockpit that exists — pooled, no writes, no console, no panels — over the real 1537 notes.

That is FEAT-0001 as already written: TASK-0001 for the edge payload, TASK-0002 for a layout that stays put while the corpus adds 634 notes a month, and a renderer. It answers three things this mock structurally cannot: does the field stay legible at real scale, does a stable layout survive real growth, and what does pooling actually cost. Every one of those is a go/no-go for the whole idea, and none of them can be answered with 80 fake cards.

What the mock is still cheap for, and worth keeping for: the arrangement rule per view. Whether Issues should sector by severity or by age needs no renderer, no real data and no performance budget — just an argument and a picture. That is the part to keep iterating here.

What is wrong with this

Depth is bad at counting

"How many issues are open?" is answered instantly by a list and badly by a field. You can see that there are several; you cannot see that there are eleven without a number in the corner — which is why one is in the corner, and why the corner number is doing more work here than the design would like to admit.

Any view whose real question is quantitative is worse in this than in the list it replaces.

Depth is bad at comparing

Two notes at different depths are different sizes, and you cannot compare two things that are not the same size. A list is uniform on purpose. Bringing two cards to the same plane to compare them is a gesture this design would have to invent, and inventing gestures is how interfaces become unlearnable.

And the honest summary

This is a reading and triage surface, not a working one. It is good at "what wants me, and what is this connected to", and worse than a list at almost everything else.

The strongest version of this proposal is not a replacement at all: it is a twelfth view in the cockpit that exists, sharing its reader, its verbs and its guards. That is a smaller claim than the one this document opened with, and it is the one I would defend.
